% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Unsupervised concept drift detectors built on reconstruction autoencoders
identify \emph{when} a high-dimensional network stream shifts, prompting recent
systems to rank individual input features by their increase in squared
reconstruction error.
In this paper, we conduct a controlled 40-seed mechanism study (20 validation
seeds + 20 held-out test seeds) across 18 synthetic and 6 real-data network
flow conditions (CICIDS2017) comparing trained autoencoder reconstruction
attribution against matched untrained networks and classical statistical
baselines.
We uncover a sharp \emph{regime dichotomy}: while training a bottleneck
autoencoder substantially improves localization of multi-feature dependency
shifts (jumping from 10.0\% to 97.5\% Recall@3), it \emph{degrades}
localization of small univariate mean shifts (scoring 32.5\% Recall@3 versus
57.5\% for an untrained random network and 100.0\% for univariate
Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests) due to bottleneck pass-through and cross-feature
decoder error smearing.
Conversely, univariate KS fails on relational shifts ($\leq 20\%$ Recall@3),
while raw Pearson correlation is at chance on univariate shifts because it is
invariant to location.
To resolve this trade-off without per-window neural retraining, we propose
\textbf{Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution} within the open-source
\textbf{Vigil} framework.
Our method pairs family-wise Bonferroni-scaled Kolmogorov-Smirnov
log-evidence (marginal pathway) with within-window van der Waerden rank-normal
(Gaussian copula) Fisher-$z$ correlation shifts (relational pathway).
Across both validation and untouched held-out test splits, Decomposed Copula-KS
achieves \textbf{100.0\% Recall@3} on every dense univariate shift in CICIDS2017
traffic (and \textbf{97.5\%--100.0\%} on synthetic univariate shifts),
\textbf{100.0\% Recall@3} on moderate-to-strong dependency shifts, and the
highest Mean Reciprocal Rank (1.000) on CICIDS2017 column permutations, where
its Recall@3 is 65\% (validation) and 55\% (held-out).
On sparse sub-population shifts, where only 5--20\% of rows change, the
univariate pathway is stronger: KS alone reaches 97.5--100.0\% Recall@3 on the
20\%-contaminated rows against 57.5--67.5\% for the decomposition, and the
calibrated peeling order recovers most of that gap (57.5\%$\to$95.0\%) but not
the 5\% case.
We then show that the asymptotic nulls behind both pathways are invalid on the
telemetry they are meant for: on 500 null draws from zero-inflated, tied,
heavy-tailed and recorded-traffic pools, the decomposed hybrid reports drift on
8.6--59.0\% of windows and the relational pathway alone on 4.8--59.0\% in which
nothing has changed, against a nominal 5\%.
Replacing the null with a pooled-label permutation null (family-wise
max-statistic $p$-values, with $\alpha$ split between the two families, which
assumes exchangeable rows within a window --- measured to matter: on raw
capture order the same null rejects in 88.7\% of no-change draws, and block
permutations reduce that only to 41.3\% at 50-row blocks) returns the rate to
3.2--5.8\%, and a greedy
edge-peeling order recovers the features
that carry a permuted-pair change on recorded traffic ($65.0\%\to95.0\%$ Recall@3),
which calibration alone does not --- at the cost of detection power on tied and
heavy-tailed pools, where the calibrated test rarely fires. The
streaming detector fixes post-drift warm-up blindness and buffer
desynchronization and reports 100.0 $\pm$ 0.0\% precision and
97.9 $\pm$ 1.9\% F1 (\texttt{verified\_only}) on a 5-seed NSL-KDD stream, an
evaluation we describe as coarse rather than conclusive: it contains five
negative chunks and a single small novel class.
Source code and provenance-verified benchmarks are available at
\url{https://github.com/Venkateswara-Sahu/OWADD}.
\end{abstract}

% ── 1. Introduction ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Machine learning models deployed in Security Operations Centers (SOCs) and
network traffic monitors face continuous non-stationarity: attack campaigns
evolve, service mixes shift, and routing changes alter baseline flow
distributions~\cite{gama2014survey}. Because ground-truth attack labels are
rarely available in real time, operators rely on unsupervised concept drift
detectors to flag distributional shifts ($P(X_t) \neq P(X_0)$) and trigger
model adaptation or analyst triage.

However, a binary drift alarm is operationally insufficient in high-dimensional
tabular streams ($d > 100$ features): analysts need \emph{feature-level drift
attribution} to understand whether an alert stems from a univariate volume
surge (a \emph{marginal shift}, such as a packet-rate spike) or a broken
protocol invariant (a \emph{relational/dependency shift}, such as unusual flag
and service co-occurrence).

Autoencoder-based drift detectors~\cite{sakurada2014anomaly,komorniczak2026owadd}
offer a seemingly natural attribution heuristic: rank each feature $j$ by its
change in mean squared reconstruction error between a reference buffer and the
current window. Despite its widespread heuristic appeal, a fundamental
scientific question has remained unanswered: \emph{Does training a bottleneck
autoencoder actually improve feature-level drift localization over untrained
random projections, input-variance heuristics, and simple nonparametric baselines?}

In this work, we answer this question through a rigorous, provenance-audited
empirical investigation and use the resulting insights to design a calibrated,
parameter-free attribution engine. Our contributions are four-fold:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Diagnosing the Autoencoder Attribution Dichotomy
        (Section~\ref{sec:diagnosis}):} Using matched initial weights across 40
        seeds on synthetic and CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids} flow
        windows, we show empirically that bottleneck autoencoder attribution exhibits
        opposing behaviors across drift regimes. Training learns cross-feature
        dependencies (improving relational shift Recall@3 by $+87.5$ percentage
        points over untrained controls), but \emph{actively harms} small
        univariate mean-shift localization ($-25.0$ percentage points vs.\
        untrained controls) because the decoder smears reconstruction error
        across correlated partner features. Furthermore, one-sided positive
        error clamping ($\max(\Delta_j, 0)$) is completely blind ($0.0\%$
        Recall@3) to variance-contraction shifts.
  \item \textbf{Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution (Section~\ref{sec:method}):}
        We show that naively combining univariate Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) tests
        with raw sample Pearson correlation fails on real network traffic
        because Fisher-$z$ is scaled by the bivariate-normal standard error while
        the true sampling variance of a correlation difference depends on fourth-order
        cumulants; heavy-tailed, zero-inflated flow features therefore produce
        spurious Fisher-$z$ spikes ($|z| > 10$) on windows in which nothing changed.
        We address marginal sensitivity and outlier distortion by transforming each
        window to empirical van der Waerden normal scores
        $\Phi^{-1}(\mathrm{rank}/(N+1))$ (a nonparametric Gaussian copula) prior to
        Fisher-$z$ scoring, placing both marginal and relational pathways on a
        unified asymptotic Bonferroni $-\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{FWE}})$ evidence scale.
  \item \textbf{Calibrated Attribution (Section~\ref{sec:calibrated}):} We show
        that the asymptotic nulls behind both pathways are invalid on
        zero-inflated, tied and heavy-tailed telemetry: on real NSL-KDD
        normal-traffic windows, where no drift has occurred, the relational
        pathway reports drift on most windows while the marginal pathway does
        not. Replacing the null with a pooled-label permutation null
        (Westfall--Young max-statistic $p$-values with $\alpha$ split between the
        two families) restores level control on exchangeable windows, and a greedy
        edge-peeling order recovers the features that carry a permuted-pair change,
        which calibration alone does not.
  \item \textbf{Streaming Continuity \& Open-Source MLOps System
        (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}):} We identify and fix two critical
        state-machine bugs in dual-autoencoder streaming detection~\cite{komorniczak2026owadd}---post-drift
        warm-up blindness and pre-adaptation buffer desynchronization---and
        provide non-adaptive frozen-reference modes (\texttt{frozen} /
        \texttt{verified\_only}) alongside adaptive updates in the open-source
        \textbf{Vigil} package (\texttt{pip install vigil-drift}).
\end{enumerate}

% ── 2. Related Work ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Univariate vs.\ Multivariate Drift Detection}
Classical streaming drift detectors such as ADWIN~\cite{bifet2007adwin},
KSWIN~\cite{raab2020kswin}, and Page-Hinkley~\cite{page1954continuous} monitor a
single scalar sequence (typically supervised prediction error) using adaptive
windowing or sequential change-point tests implemented in libraries such as
\texttt{river}~\cite{montiel2021river}. In label-free high-dimensional settings,
Komorniczak~\cite{komorniczak2026owadd} introduced Open World Autoencoding
Drift Detection (OWADD), pairing an adaptive autoencoder ($A$) with a frozen
known-class mirror autoencoder ($A_{\mathrm{KC}}$), a replicated Welch T-test on
reconstruction errors, and Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)~\cite{scott1992multivariate}
for novel-class recognition. However, OWADD provides no mechanism to attribute
detected drift to specific input features.

\subsection{Feature Attribution for Concept Drift}
Post-hoc model explainers such as SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} and
LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016lime} explain individual supervised predictions rather
than distributional shifts between two unlabeled sample windows. Hinder et
al.~\cite{hinder2023explain} frame drift explanation by applying model
explanations to proxy classifiers trained between windows. In autoencoder
monitoring, practitioners
commonly inspect per-feature squared reconstruction residuals
$(x_{ij} - \hat{x}_{ij})^2$, assuming that features with the largest residual
increase caused the shift; game-theoretic Shapley explanations of reconstruction
anomalies are studied for autoencoders by Antwarg et al.~\cite{antwarg2021explaining}
and for PCA reconstruction residuals by Takeishi~\cite{takeishi2019reconstruction}.
We benchmark raw reconstruction-residual ranking against matched untrained
autoencoders and calibrated nonparametric tests on network intrusion streams; the
untrained-weight control is what separates \emph{learning} from the residual
statistic itself.

\subsection{Marginal and Relational Drift Statistics}
Per-feature Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests with a Bonferroni correction are the
standard univariate baseline for dataset-shift detection, benchmarked at scale
by Rabanser et al.~\cite{rabanser2019failing}. Our marginal pathway is therefore
an instance of a known strong baseline, which is why we report it separately
rather than as a contribution. The relational pathway instead tests change in
the copula: normal scores are the nonparanormal construction of Liu et
al.~\cite{liu2009nonparanormal}, the target quantity (change in the covariance
structure between two samples) is the two-sample covariance problem of Cai et
al.~\cite{cai2013twosample}, with the classical asymptotics for comparing two
correlation matrices due to Jennrich~\cite{jennrich1970asymptotic} and
Steiger~\cite{steiger1980testing}; kernel two-sample
tests~\cite{gretton2012kernel} are the standard alternative when the shift is
not low-dimensional. What
distinguishes our setting is \emph{localisation}: we need a per-feature ranking,
not a single test statistic, and the ranking is a vertex-cover problem on the
significant edges rather than a maximum. For that step the relevant machinery is
resampling-based multiple testing with a max-statistic null~\cite{westfall1993resampling},
which we adopt in Section~\ref{sec:calibrated}.

The drift-detection literature these tests sit in is surveyed by Lu et
al.~\cite{lu2018learning} and Webb et al.~\cite{webb2018analysing}; the
information-theoretic and distributional change detectors of Kifer et
al.~\cite{kifer2004detecting} and Dasu et al.~\cite{dasu2006information} are the
two-sample precedents for treating a window pair as the unit of evidence. For
the feature-level question see also Hinder et al.~\cite{hinder2023explain}. A
consequence we lean on later is that window-level tests need enough rows per
window to resolve small sub-population shifts, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} reports power as a function of the shifted-row
fraction rather than only of shift magnitude.

% ── 3. Diagnosing Autoencoder Attribution Failure Modes ──────────────────────
\section{Why Autoencoder Attribution Fails on Univariate Shifts}
\label{sec:diagnosis}

Let $X_{\mathrm{ref}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{ref}} \times d}$ and
$X_{\mathrm{cur}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{cur}} \times d}$ denote reference
and current windows, and let $h_\theta(x) = g_\phi(f_\psi(x))$ denote a
bottleneck autoencoder with latent dimension $m < d$. The per-feature mean
squared reconstruction error change is:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_j = \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{cur}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{cur}}}
             \left(x^{\mathrm{cur}}_{ij} - \hat{x}^{\mathrm{cur}}_{ij}\right)^2
           - \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{ref}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{\mathrm{ref}}}
             \left(x^{\mathrm{ref}}_{ij} - \hat{x}^{\mathrm{ref}}_{ij}\right)^2
  \label{eq:ae_delta}
\end{equation}

Our controlled experiments comparing a trained autoencoder ($h_{\theta^*}$)
against its exact initial random-weight clone ($h_{\theta_0}$) reveal three
structural failure modes of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ae_delta}:

\subsection{Failure Mode 1: One-Sided Sign Blindness}
When feature $j$ undergoes a variance contraction ($X^{\mathrm{cur}}_{\cdot, j} = \gamma X^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\cdot, j}$ with $\gamma < 1$), its values concentrate near the mean, causing its reconstruction residual to \emph{decrease} ($\Delta_j < 0$). Ranking rules that clamp negative deltas to zero ($\max(\Delta_j, 0)$) assign zero contribution to the shifted feature, resulting in \textbf{0.0\% Recall@3} on synthetic variance contractions (\path{independent/std_0.5}).

\subsection{Failure Mode 2: Bottleneck Pass-Through \& Error Smearing}
Even when using two-sided absolute deltas ($|\Delta_j|$), training the
autoencoder on reference traffic \emph{reduces} Recall@3 on small univariate
mean shifts (\path{independent/mean_0.5}) from \textbf{57.5\%} (untrained
random weights) to \textbf{32.5\%} (trained weights), whereas simple squared-input
change achieves 62.5\% and univariate KS achieves 100.0\%.
Why does learning hurt univariate localization? During training, the encoder-decoder
pair $g_\phi \circ f_\psi$ learns a low-rank projection spanning the principal
directions of $X_{\mathrm{ref}}$. In the linear-autoencoder (rank-$m$ PCA)
limit with orthogonal projection $P \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, adding a mean
shift $\mu e_j$ shifts the mean residual vector by $(I - P)\mu e_j$: feature $j$'s
expected residual shift scales with $\mu^2 (1 - P_{jj})^2$ (attenuated whenever
$P_{jj}$ is large, i.e., bottleneck pass-through), while every correlated partner
$k \neq j$ receives $\mu^2 P_{kj}^2$ (cross-feature error smearing).

\subsection{Failure Mode 3: Raw Correlation Is Uncalibrated on Heavy-Tailed Flows}
On multivariate dependency shifts where marginals remain $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$,
univariate KS fails ($\leq 20\%$ Recall@3) while Pearson correlation change
succeeds. This suggests combining KS and Pearson correlation, and we test that
hybrid directly (Section~\ref{sec:calibration}). Two things go wrong on real
flows.

First, sample Pearson correlation is invariant to location and positive scale, so on
univariate mean or scale shifts it contributes a ranking near the uniform-random
Recall@3 baseline $3/d = 15.0\%$ for $d = 20$ ($7.5\%$--$17.5\%$ in
Table~\ref{tab:attribution_study}), which reflects location/scale invariance rather
than heavy-tail damage.
Second, the damage shows up as \emph{false} relational rejections, which is a
calibration question and has to be measured on windows in which nothing has
changed. There the raw-statistic pathway is the worst performer: on 500 real
null windows it rejects 98.4\% of the time, against 82.2\% for the copula
variant and 0.4\% for the marginal test (Table~\ref{tab:real-null}). That is the
empirical form of the theoretical worry: Fisher's $z$ has a variance that depends
on fourth-order cumulants, network flow features have $\mathbb{E}[X_j^4] \gg 3$
and large point masses, so unshifted pairs produce $|z| > 10$ as sampling noise.

% ── 4. Method: Vigil with Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution ───────────────────
\section{Method: Vigil \& Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Streaming Dual-Autoencoder \& Replicated Welch Detection}
Vigil maintains two autoencoders with three ReLU hidden layers of width $h = 10$
($d \to h \to h \to h \to d$): an adaptive autoencoder $A$ and a frozen
known-class reference mirror $A_{\mathrm{KC}}$.
For each incoming chunk $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, we compute sample
reconstruction errors $E_A(X_t)$ and perform $r = 15$ bootstrap replications
of Welch's unequal-variance two-sample T-test ($s = 30$ samples per replication)
against the reference error buffer $\mathcal{B}_E$. Drift is declared when the
fraction of replications with $p_k < \alpha$ ($\alpha = 0.05$) reaches
threshold $\delta = 0.30$.

\textbf{Fixing Post-Drift Warm-Up Blindness \& Buffer Desynchronization:}
In the reference OWADD design~\cite{komorniczak2026owadd}, resetting
$\mathcal{B}_E$ upon drift when $|\mathcal{B}_E|_{\max} > n$ caused the detector
to re-enter initial warm-up for $\lceil |\mathcal{B}_E|_{\max} / n \rceil$
subsequent chunks, blinding the detector to consecutive attacks. Furthermore,
storing pre-update errors $E_A^{\mathrm{pre}}(X_t)$ in $\mathcal{B}_E$ before
fine-tuning $A$ created a scale mismatch against post-update errors on chunk
$t+1$. Vigil fixes both flaws by tracking initial warm-up completion separately
from post-drift buffer resets and synchronizing $\mathcal{B}_E$ with
post-adaptation errors $E_A^{\mathrm{post}}(X_t)$ (Algorithm~\ref{alg:vigil}).
When automatic retraining on unverified traffic is undesirable, Vigil provides
non-adaptive modes (\path{adaptation_mode="frozen"} and its SOC alias
\path{verified_only}) that keep $A$ and the attribution reference batch
$R_X$ fixed while sliding $\mathcal{B}_E$ only on non-drifted post-warmup chunks.

\subsection{Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution (DDA)}
To capture both univariate shifts and multivariate dependency shifts without
per-window neural retraining, we decompose feature attribution into two
pathways placed on a common Bonferroni log-evidence scale:

\subsubsection{Marginal Pathway (\texorpdfstring{$E_{\mathrm{marg}}$}{E\_marg})}
For each feature $j \in \{1, \dots, d\}$, we compute the exact two-sample
Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic $D_{\mathrm{KS}, j}$ and two-sided $p$-value
$p_{\mathrm{KS}, j}$ between $X_{\mathrm{ref}, \cdot, j}$ and
$X_{\mathrm{cur}, \cdot, j}$. Applying a Bonferroni correction across $d$
features (plus a $10^{-3} D_{\mathrm{KS}, j}$ tie-breaker for machine-precision
saturated shifts) yields the non-negative marginal log-evidence:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\mathrm{marg}}(j) = \max\left(0,\; -\log_{10}\left(
    \min\left(1,\; d \cdot p_{\mathrm{KS}, j}\right)
  \right)\right) + 10^{-3} D_{\mathrm{KS}, j}
  \label{eq:e_marg}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Relational Copula Pathway (\texorpdfstring{$E_{\mathrm{rel}}$}{E\_rel})}
To eliminate heavy-tailed kurtosis distortion and guarantee strict invariance to
any monotonic marginal transformation, we transform each feature $j$ within
each window $W \in \{X_{\mathrm{ref}}, X_{\mathrm{cur}}\}$ of size $N$ into its
van der Waerden empirical normal score (nonparametric Gaussian copula
projection):
\begin{equation}
  Z^{(W)}_{ij} = \Phi^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathrm{rank}(W_{ij})}{N + 1}\right)
  \label{eq:copula}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard normal quantile function. Because every
non-constant column of $Z^{(W)}$ has an empirical normal marginal distribution,
its sample Pearson correlation matrix $R^{(W)} = \mathrm{Corr}(Z^{(W)})$
measures pure rank-copula dependency, and the Fisher-$z$ statistic between reference
and current windows is scaled by
$\sigma_z = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{ref}} - 3} + \frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{cur}} - 3}}$,
the standard error that Fisher-$z$ has for \emph{bivariate normal} data:
\begin{equation}
  z_{jk} = \frac{\left|\operatorname{arctanh}(R^{\mathrm{cur}}_{jk})
                 - \operatorname{arctanh}(R^{\mathrm{ref}}_{jk})\right|}{\sigma_z},
  \quad j \neq k
  \label{eq:fisher_z}
\end{equation}
Two caveats matter, and Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} measures both. The normal
scores make each \emph{marginal} Gaussian (up to ties), not the \emph{joint} law:
for a non-Gaussian copula, and for columns with ties (average ranks give point
masses rather than a continuous normal law), $\sigma_z$ is not the sampling
standard error of the difference. And taking a maximum over $d-1$ dependent
edges before a Bonferroni correction over $M$ pairs is not an exact family-wise
level. We therefore treat the asymptotic scaling as a fast approximation whose
validity is an empirical question, and report its measured false-alarm rate.
Across all $M = \binom{d}{2}$ feature pairs, let $z_j^{\max} = \max_{k \neq j} z_{jk}$
and $\mathrm{RMS}(z_j) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k \neq j} z_{jk}^2}$. We convert
$z_j^{\max}$ to two-sided Bonferroni log-evidence across $M$ pairs,
plus a small hub tie-breaker $\epsilon \cdot \mathrm{RMS}(z_j)$ ($\epsilon = 10^{-3}$)
that prioritizes a permuted ``hub'' feature over an unshifted partner when both
share the same maximum pairwise edge $z_{jk}$:
\begin{align}
  E_{\mathrm{rel}}(j) &= \max\left(0,\; -\log_{10}\left(
    \min\left(1,\; 2 M \cdot \Phi(-z_j^{\max})\right)
  \right)\right) \nonumber \\
  &\quad + \epsilon \cdot \mathrm{RMS}(z_j)
  \label{eq:e_rel}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Unified Synthesis}
Because both $E_{\mathrm{marg}}(j)$ and $E_{\mathrm{rel}}(j)$ express
Bonferroni-scaled $-\log_{10}(p_{\mathrm{FWE}})$ surprise in comparable units,
the unified score and dominant drift mechanism for feature $j$ are:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{DDA}}(j) = \max\bigl(E_{\mathrm{marg}}(j), E_{\mathrm{rel}}(j)\bigr),
  \;
  c_j = \frac{S_{\mathrm{DDA}}(j)}{\sum_{l=1}^d S_{\mathrm{DDA}}(l)}
  \label{eq:dda_combined}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{type}(j) = \texttt{relational}$ if
$E_{\mathrm{rel}}(j) > E_{\mathrm{marg}}(j)$ and $\texttt{marginal}$ otherwise.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Vigil Streaming Detection \& Drift Attribution}
\label{alg:vigil}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Chunk $X_t \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$, models $A, A_{\mathrm{KC}}$,
         error buffer $\mathcal{B}_E$, reference batch $R_X$, threshold $\delta$,
         adaptation mode $m \in \{\texttt{auto}, \texttt{frozen}, \texttt{verified\_only}\}$
\STATE $E_A^{\mathrm{pre}} \leftarrow \|X_t - A(X_t)\|^2_2$, \quad
       $E_{\mathrm{KC}} \leftarrow \|X_t - A_{\mathrm{KC}}(X_t)\|^2_2$
\STATE $\mathrm{ready} \leftarrow \mathrm{IsReady}(\mathcal{B}_E)$
\STATE $\mathrm{drift}, \mathrm{sev} \leftarrow \mathrm{WelchReplicatedTest}(E_A^{\mathrm{pre}}, \mathcal{B}_E, \delta)$
\STATE $\mathrm{novelty} \leftarrow \mathrm{KDEDetect}(E_{\mathrm{KC}})$ \COMMENT{optional \texttt{upper\_tail\_only} guard}
\IF{drift}
  \STATE $\mathrm{attr} \leftarrow \mathrm{Attribute}(R_X, X_t)$ \COMMENT{\texttt{decomposed} or \texttt{calibrated}}
  \IF{$m = \texttt{auto}$}
    \STATE $\mathrm{FineTune}(A, X_t)$
    \STATE $\mathcal{B}_E \leftarrow \|X_t - A(X_t)\|^2_2$, \quad $R_X \leftarrow X_t$
  \ENDIF
\ELSIF{$\mathrm{ready} \;\wedge\; m \in \{\texttt{frozen}, \texttt{verified\_only}\}$}
  \STATE $\mathrm{AppendFIFO}(\mathcal{B}_E, E_A^{\mathrm{pre}})$
\ENDIF
\RETURN drift, sev, novelty, attr
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ── 6. Experiments ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

We evaluate Vigil across three complementary protocols: (1)~a provenance-audited,
40-seed feature attribution benchmark (20 validation seeds + 20 held-out test
seeds) across 18 synthetic and 6 real CICIDS2017~\cite{sharafaldin2018cicids}
conditions (\path{experiments/decomposed_study.py}), (2)~a 500-trial null-calibration,
65-cell localisation, power, and block-exchangeability study
(\path{experiments/calibrated_study.py}, \path{real_null_calibration.py},
\path{calibrated_blocknull.py}), and (3)~a 5-seed,
50-chunk non-stationary intrusion stream on NSL-KDD~\cite{tavallaee2009nsl}
comparing Vigil against classical \texttt{river} detectors under both supervised
and unsupervised error streams (\path{experiments/nsl_kdd_multiseed.py}).
The CICIDS2017 arrays are prepared with the protocol of Engelen et
al.~\cite{engelen2021troubleshooting} as implemented in
\path{experiments/datasets/cicids2017_improved.py}: rows with a non-finite
feature are excluded, duplicate flows are collapsed by content identity (keeping
the earliest timestamp), scaling is min--max fitted on Monday only with no
clipping or imputation, and the day split is Monday (reference) / Tuesday
(validation) / Wednesday--Friday (held-out test). The calibration study of
Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} reads those prepared arrays rather than raw
captures.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Attribution Benchmark (Synthetic \& CICIDS2017 Validation + Held-Out Test Splits)}

\textbf{Protocol:} Following our frozen mechanism protocol, we evaluate a
20-seed \textbf{Validation Split} (seeds $50\dots 69$) and a strictly untouched
20-seed \textbf{Held-Out Test Split} (seeds $70\dots 89$) on $d = 20$ features.
In each trial, two ground-truth features $(j_1^*, j_2^*)$ are randomly selected
and perturbed. The synthetic suite covers univariate mean shifts ($+0.5, +2.0$),
scale shifts ($\times 0.5, \times 2.0$), correlation induction ($0.8$ in an
independent background), and a 12-condition dependency grid varying background
correlation ($\rho_0 \in \{0.0, 0.3\}$), window size ($n \in \{200, 800\}$), and
local correlation increment ($\Delta\rho \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.6\}$).
For real network traffic, the Validation Split uses 32,000 disjoint flows from
Tuesday of the improved CICIDS2017 dataset, while the Held-Out Test Split uses
32,000 untouched flows from Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday ($n_{\mathrm{ref}} =
n_{\mathrm{cur}} = 800$ per seed). Both splits are standardized strictly by
Monday normal training statistics (2,000 separate flows, zero split overlap),
injecting controlled two-feature shifts: \texttt{mean\_0.5}, \texttt{mean\_2},
\texttt{std\_0.5}, \texttt{std\_2}, and \texttt{permutation} (which preserves
exact univariate marginals while breaking inter-feature dependencies). We report
Mean \textbf{Recall@3 (\%)} (fraction of the two true shifted features recovered
in the top 3) and \textbf{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}.

moderate-to-strong dependency shifts, but the comparison is not uniformly
favourable, and we report the cells where it loses rather than the best metric:
a single-pathway baseline scores higher in 18 of the 65 drifted localisation
cells of Table~\ref{tab:localization}, and
the gaps fall into two systematic families. On sparse sub-population marginal
shifts (5--20\% of rows) univariate KS reaches 97.5--100.0\% Recall@3 where the
decomposition reaches 57.5--67.5\%, because spurious uncalibrated relational
scores outrank weak marginal evidence; on weak $0.2\sigma$ mean shifts KS reaches
70.0--72.5\% against 40.0--55.0\%. The calibrated peeling order of
Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} recovers most of the 20\%-contamination cells
(57.5\%$\to$95.0\%, 60.0\%$\to$95.0\%, 67.5\%$\to$90.0\%) but not the 5\% cells,
and the remaining cells are small gaps (2.5--7.5 points) confined to the
tied and heavy-tailed populations. The dependency rows also mix:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Univariate Marginal Shifts:} For small $+0.5\sigma$ mean shifts across synthetic, validation, and held-out test flows, Decomposed Copula-KS reaches \textbf{100.0\% Recall@3}, beating two-sided trained AE attribution by $+67.5$ points (95\% CI: $+50.0$ to $+82.5$) on synthetic data, $+80.0$ points ($+67.5$ to $+92.5$) on Tuesday validation flows, and \textbf{$+92.5$ points ($+85.0$ to $+100.0$)} on Wednesday--Friday test flows.
  \item \textbf{Multivariate Dependency Shifts:} On synthetic correlation and dependency shifts ($\Delta\rho = 0.6$ or $n = 800$), Decomposed Copula-KS achieves \textbf{100.0\% Recall@3}, beating univariate KS by $+80.0$ to $+97.5$ percentage points.
  \item \textbf{CICIDS2017 Column Permutation:} Under real flow column permutation, where univariate marginals remain unchanged, Decomposed Copula-KS achieves \textbf{65.0\%} validation Recall@3 ($+37.5$ points over two-sided trained AE, 95\% CI $[+22.5, +52.5]$) and \textbf{55.0\%} held-out test Recall@3 ($+37.5$ points, 95\% CI $[+20.0, +50.1]$). Its MRR is \textbf{1.000} on both splits against raw Pearson's \textbf{0.775}
(Val) and \textbf{0.700} (Test), i.e.\ one of the two permuted features is always
ranked first. Recall@3 tells a different story on the held-out split: raw Pearson
reaches \textbf{67.5\%} against \textbf{55.0\%} for Decomposed Copula-KS, because a
max-edge aggregation ranks the permuted feature's partner next to it and MRR, which
only inspects the top entry, does not see that. Covering \emph{both} permuted
features is the harder criterion, and Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} shows the
edge-peeling order that recovers it.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Why Decomposed Copula-KS Succeeds Without Neural Retraining:}
A key practical advantage of Decomposed Copula-KS is that it decouples
\emph{detection} (where the dual autoencoder captures holistic nonlinear
deviations from the normal manifold) from \emph{feature localization} (where
two-sample KS and rank-normal copula tests isolate marginal vs.\
relational shifts at a measured \textbf{7.6\,ms} median per window at
$d = 20$, $n = 400$ and \textbf{30.0\,ms} at $d = 78$, $n = 400$ on a laptop
CPU; the permutation-calibrated variant of Section~\ref{sec:calibrated} costs
$B$ such passes, measured at \textbf{0.30--0.38\,s} (\textbf{1.00--1.33\,s} at
$d = 78$) median per call at $B = 199$, \texttt{artifacts/benchmarks/}).

\textbf{Scope and Interpretation Boundaries:}
Following strict scientific reporting standards, we emphasize four boundaries:
(1)~Our real-data attribution benchmarks on CICIDS2017 inject controlled
two-feature shifts into reserved validation and held-out test flows; Recall@3
and MRR measure recovery of perturbed feature indices rather than supervised
intrusion classification or formal interventional causal discovery.
(2)~When a single feature column is permuted in a correlated network dataset,
its pairwise correlations with multiple unshifted partner columns change
simultaneously; thus, while our hub tie-breaker achieves 100\% Top-1 accuracy
(MRR = 1.000) across both validation and held-out test splits, the third ranked
slot can still include a strongly coupled partner feature (yielding 65.0\% Val
and 55.0\% Test Recall@3 under max-edge ranking, resolved to 95.0\% at $n = 400$
by greedy edge peeling).
(3)~A permuted feature with no dependence on the other columns is not
identifiable from a correlation test at all; the calibrated pathway reports
$\max_{k \ne j}|R^{\mathrm{ref}}_{jk}|$ alongside its ranking so such features can
be recognized instead of silently ranked.
(4)~The uncalibrated decomposition is the weaker choice for \emph{sparse sub-population}
marginal shifts and for very small ($0.2\sigma$) mean shifts: at 5--20\%
contamination a univariate KS ranking beats it by 5--42 points, and at 5\%
contamination the calibrated order loses detection power while holding nominal
false-alarm control. On tied, zero-inflated telemetry the calibrated test fires
at the nominal rate on weak permutations, so its lower recall there reflects
missing detection power rather than a ranking failure.

\textbf{What the streaming evaluation can and cannot support.}
The NSL-KDD stream is a batch-anomaly task, not a sequence of stationary
segments: the ground truth is ``chunk index $> 5$'' against a normal-only
reference, so there are only \textbf{five negative chunks}, a trivial
always-alarm detector reaches 94.7\% F1 on that accounting, and
\texttt{verified\_only} never adapts, which makes it a batch detector rather than
a drift-following one. Several rare known-attack classes (\texttt{spy}: 2 rows,
\texttt{perl}: 3, \texttt{phf}: 4) and the alphabetically selected novel class
(\texttt{warezmaster}: 20 unique rows) are resampled with replacement, and the
change-point baselines (ADWIN, KSWIN, Page--Hinkley) run at default parameters
on an error scale of $10^{-2}$. We therefore report the streaming numbers as a
state-machine continuity check rather than evidence that the detector tracks
multi-regime concept drift; a piecewise-stationary stream with several change
points, null streams, and tuned baselines is the evaluation that stronger claim
would require.

\textbf{What the calibration study covers.}
The null and localisation experiments use $d = 20$ and window sizes of 400--800
rows from the prepared CICIDS2017 splits (Monday-fitted standardisation,
Tuesday validation, Wednesday--Friday held-out), not raw packet captures, and the
synthetic populations are constructed to be adversarial rather than realistic.
The permutation null assumes exchangeability of pooled rows within a window
pair, which autocorrelated streams violate: on CICIDS2017 Tuesday in recorded
order, both windows of a genuinely null pair are drawn from one recorded block
and the calibrated test still rejects in $88.7\%$ of trials at block size $1$,
$69.3\%$ at $10$, $50.0\%$ at $25$ and $41.3\%$ at $50$, while the i.i.d.\ pools
sit at $1.7$--$4.0\%$ and a $+0.5\sigma$ single-feature mean shift is declared in
$100\%$ of trials on recorded pools ($92.3$--$99.3\%$ on synthetic pools) at every
block size (Table~\ref{tab:block-null}). Blocking reduces the inflation but does
not restore the level, because the measured dependence extends past fifty rows, so
window-pair $p$-values computed on capture order must not be quoted as calibrated.
Real-data rows of the calibration tables resample a fixed set of disjoint blocks
and use the normal-only NSL-KDD pool, so the block-clustered interval, not the
binomial one, is the interval to quote there.

% ── 8. Conclusion ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

This study investigated autoencoder-based drift attribution in network streams
across 40 seeds, and found empirically that bottleneck autoencoders suffer from
univariate pass-through blindness and cross-feature error smearing while
classical univariate tests fail on relational shifts. To resolve this dichotomy,
we introduced \textbf{Decomposed Copula-KS Attribution} in \textbf{Vigil},
unifying KS marginal evidence with Gaussian-copula Fisher-$z$ relational
evidence. Across 920 scored attribution trials (23 drifted conditions, each run on
the validation and held-out splits with 20 seeds per condition--split pair; the
no-injection control is excluded), it localizes dense marginal and
relational features at or above the
strongest single-pathway baseline, while on sparse sub-population marginal
shifts the univariate path stays ahead (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and the
dependency rows show the ordering failure that
motivates the permutation-calibrated, edge-peeling variant of
Section~\ref{sec:calibrated}: on tied and heavy-tailed telemetry the asymptotic
nulls reject 45.8--59.0\% of null windows (82.6\% on normal-only NSL-KDD) at a
nominal 5\%, and the permutation null returns that rate to 3.2--5.8\% (3.4\% on
NSL-KDD) while edge peeling lifts permuted-pair Recall@3 from 65.0\% to 95.0\%.
The streaming detector delivers
$97.9 \pm 1.9\%$ (frozen/verified-only) and $89.9 \pm 2.9\%$ (adaptive) F1 on the
5-seed NSL-KDD stream, where the evaluation is too coarse to support a
stronger claim (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).
